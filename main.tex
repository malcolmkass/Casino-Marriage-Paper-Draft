\begin{abstract}
IN PROGRESS, DO NOT CITE: Casino development is often promoted as an engine of local economic growth, but its broader social consequences remain less understood. This paper investigates how casino openings reshape demographic patterns and family-supporting institutions in U.S. counties from 2003 to 2023. Exploiting staggered treatment timing in a difference-in-differences framework, we document consistent evidence that casinos erode community structures. We estimate 0.90 and 1.10 percentage-point declines in fertility rates for women and men, respectively, associated with an estimated drop of 37 children per 1000 women and 45 per 1000 men. These demographic shifts are reflected in reduced school-age populations, especially in public schools, and in declines in religious adherence. The results demonstrate that casinos may generate social capital losses alongside changes in economic activity, altering the composition of local populations and undermining institutions historically central to family and community life.   
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\doublespacing
%Kass:  Global vs. Local effects of casinos

Local community officials often credit new casinos as significant economic drivers that, in turn, attract economic inflows to the local area. \parencite{keranews2025irvingcasino} However, negative attention has come to light regarding the effects of gambling on local communities. While the gambling industry generates some economic benefits, often stemming from localized increases in employment and tax revenues, evidence is accumulating of its negative spillovers on social strata. These effects are exacerbated due to the addictive nature of risky gambling behavior. For example, since the advent of online gambling, researchers estimate that only 4\% of individuals gain income from gambling and 5.3\% gamble more than 10\% of their monthly income. \parencite{Clark2024LegalizedGambling}  Gambling may also dampen local population growth, which is particularly concerning to officials. Communities may wish to promote inbound migration and fertility as sources of economic growth. Hence, researchers need to determine whether sin industries, such as gambling, generate these negative effects on community and family formation, including fertility. 


Our interests are two-fold. First, motivated by the current situation with a potential casino resort in proximity to our academic institution, the University of Dallas, we focus on tabula rasa situations in which the first-mover casino resort enters a community without a prior casino or gambling presence in the immediate or surrounding vicinity. Hence, our questions of interest differ from those in research on "entertainment districts," where economic theory may be less clear about the magnitude of potential outcomes. Diminishing returns suggest larger effects for these first-mover casinos, for better or worse. Second, we examine the effects of casinos on family formation and fertility, as well as on community institutions, such as schools and religious institutions. Casinos represent a significant change to local communities, yet their effects on family formation and community institutions remain relatively unexplored. Current family formation research relies either on descriptive event analysis studies that focus on a single region or on studies that lack key covariates, potentially limiting coefficient precision. \parencite{moellman2013indian}  



This paper provides the first comprehensive analysis of how casino openings affect marriage, fertility, population composition, and family-supporting institutions using quasi-experimental variation from staggered casino openings across U.S. counties. We constructed a specialized dataset of casino openings across the United States matched to American Community Survey (ACS) microdata from 2000 to 2023 for family formation and education outcomes, and Religious Congregations \& Membership Studies data from 2000, 2010, and 2020 for religious adherence. We employ staggered Difference-in-Differences treatment timing to estimate causal effects. We find that the presence of a first-mover casino harms population growth trajectories in the local community compared with control areas without a casino, primarily by reducing the number of children. We do not find changes in age-adjusted marital rates for women or men. Still, we observe declines in the external margin (percentage of families with children) and the internal margin (number of children per family). Downstream effects of these demographic shifts include reduced school enrollment and religious adherence, as well as diminished Catholic identity among the local population.

\section{Background}
\subsection{Economic activity and Family and Community Formation Structures}

We define factors that contribute to family structures as marriage/cohabitation formations and fertility rates. Community structures are institutions that help build social capital, a key factor in helping families raise successful children, especially among lower-income families. \parencite{chetty2026opportunity} Development of social capital is tied to family participation in formal public and private education and religious institutions. 

Scholars identify several economic changes associated with new economic activity that may contribute to family formation and fertility. These include incomes, property values, employment level, and wages. Looking at fertility through the lens of consumption, positive income shocks drive fertility, with findings ranging from North Dakota's Natural Gas boom  \parencite{kearney2018male} to commodity shocks driving fertility in oil-rich areas of Chile. \parencite{gallego2023baby} With this evidence, scholars speculate that the income effect of these shocks overwhelms a possible negative substitution effect due to an increase in the opportunity cost of fertility due to more advantageous economic opportunities. Viewing fertility as an investment, contrary to historical trends, increases the marginal propensity to save, which parents may view children as a means of smoothing future consumption. \parencite{goldin2021career,doepke2023economics}  



\subsection{Casinos and Economic and Social Outcomes}



Casinos have significant effects on local communities, with uncertain impacts on family formation and community institutions. Casinos have been found to increase tax revenues, employment, earnings, and property values. Several papers find increases in local tax revenues and local economic activity. \parencite{humphreys2013new,wiley2011casino} However, many of these benefits found in the previous literature appear to be only a step-wise increase in growth, with limited spillovers primarily to the hospitality and service industries.\footnote{The economic impacts from entertainment and tourism industries sharply diminish from their initial investment. Once the venue is established, it is difficult to expand gaming production with new capital investments compared to manufacturing and other industries. Others find that casinos do not materialize in today's entertainment-saturated environment because they are unable to generate new spending in a community, rather than merely substituting for spending at other local entertainment venues. \parencite{Scavette2022Casinos,Krutz2022Casinos} Casinos have been found to increase employment, with mixed evidence on wages; some studies find employment gains without wage improvements. \parencite{cotti2008effect} Lastly, research has found that casinos can also increase property values for local retail businesses in their vicinity, particularly when located in a larger entertainment district. \parencite{wiley2011casino} Others find that these employment benefits, unsurprisingly, are centered in the hospitality and service industries. \parencite{humphreys2013new}.}



However, these benefits often don't outweigh the negative externalities associated with a casino's presence, such as increased crime. \parencite{Soriano2025Why, Scavette2022Casinos} Outside of some isolated cases of community welfare improvements in rural areas, \parencite{moellman2013indian}, there is an array of research findings that casinos generate negative externalities on local communities, primarily crime. The seminal paper in this regard is Grinols et al. (2006), who find that casinos generate an 8.6\% increase in property crime and a 12.6\% increase in violent crime in casino-location counties. \parencite{grinols2006casinos}. These socially negative outcomes do not occur immediately but occur after a lag of 3-4 years. Mechanisms involved include that casinos generate high concentrations of easy targets. Generally, people holding significant amounts of cash are located in concentrated areas.
In addition, the income-reducing effects of casinos impoverish the lower-income segments of society by lowering the already low opportunity cost of crime. \parencite{Clark2024LegalizedGambling}  Outside of financial considerations, regarding the gaming activity itself,  gambling as a form of entertainment may cause a time substitution effect for parents, substituting away from family-supporting activities. Cohabitation, marriage, and family formation depend on available resources such as income, as well as effort and time. Casinos strain these resources due to the partner's absence from the household. Family partnerships are strong coordinating devices, reducing communication and search costs and mitigating financial risk by pooling resources. \parencite{kearney2023two} Hence, while decreases in income and crime are clear concerns, time substitution of gambling for child-rearing activities may compound and delay family formation. Lastly, some have concerns that, given the strong social norms tied to family and community formation, family- and community-supporting choices do not actually map to family- and community-preferred choices, highlighting that choices under strong social norms are often susceptible to the weak axiom of revealed-preference violations. \parencite{sen1973behaviour} However, evidence suggests that these behaviors correlate to stated preferences. \parencite{muller2022illusion}

\section{Empirical Strategy}

\subsection{Two-Way Fixed Effects}

We begin with a standard two-way fixed effects (TWFE) specification:

\begin{equation}
Y_{ct} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Post}_{ct} + \mathbf{X}_{ct}'\gamma + \mu_c + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{ct}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Y_{ct}$ is the average number of children for women in county $c$ and year $t$, $\text{Post}_{ct}$ is an indicator equal to one if county $c$ has an operating casino in year $t$, $\mathbf{X}_{ct}$ includes demographic controls (average age and age squared), $\mu_c$ are county fixed effects, $\lambda_t$ are year fixed effects, and $\varepsilon_{ct}$ is the error term. We cluster standard errors at the county level and weight by population. The advantages of this method include tractability and the ability to control for time-invariant county-specific characteristics.

\subsection{Identification Strategy}

Counties chosen for casino openings would be regarded as selection on fixed effects. Our identification relies on the assumption that, conditional on controls and fixed effects, the timing of casino openings is quasi-random with respect to population and fertility trends. \footnote{This is called the independence assumption: that the treatment is assigned to a population independent of the population's potential outcomes.} While casino placement is endogenous to economic conditions, we argue that the specific timing of openings—often determined by lengthy regulatory processes, ballot initiatives, and legal challenges—provides plausibly exogenous variation in treatment timing. Households and families may choose to enter or exit a location due to the presence of a casino, but differences in family and community outcomes do not seem to matter with casino location. 
Generally, to causally identify our parameters of interest, we need to determine whether the sources of bias are a concern. From a rudimentary point of view, only examining ex post observations, we have the following decomposition of the Average Treatment effect:

\begin{align*}
\underbrace{E[Y^1] - E[Y^0]}_{\text{Average Treatment Effect}}
\;=\;
& \underbrace{E_N[Y \mid D = 1] - E_N[Y \mid D = 0]}_{\text{Simple difference in outcomes (SDO)}} \\
& - \underbrace{E[Y^0 \mid D = 1] - E[Y^0 \mid D = 0]}_{\text{Unknown Selection bias}} \\[4pt]
& - \underbrace{(1 - \pi)(ATT - ATU)}_{\text{Heterogenous treatment effect bias}}
\end{align*}
Where:
\begin{itemize}
\item SDO: Ex post outcome difference
\item $1-\pi$: Weight for the Untreated observations 
\item ATT: Average Treatment Effect of treated
\item ATU: Average Treatment Effect of Untreated
\end{itemize}
The difference in ex post results is the difference between ex post treatment and control observations. The sources of selection bias are that we do not observe the treated counties as if no casino was located in that county whereas we have outcomes from our the control counties.  However, this may be mitigated since the treatment assignment mechanism is not driven by the values of the dependent variable. The source of heterogeneous treatment effect bias is that treated units may be more responsive to treatment than control units if they were treated.
However, given that we are most interested in the ATU, we rearrange our potential outcomes equation to isolate the ATU on the RHS. 
\begin{align*}
\underbrace{E[Y^1 \mid D = 0] - E[Y^0 \mid D = 0]}_{\text{ATU (target estimand)}} 
\;=\; & \phantom{+}\underbrace{E_N[Y \mid D = 1] - E_N[Y \mid D = 0]}_{\text{Ex post outcome difference (SDO)}}\\
& - \underbrace{E[Y^0 \mid D = 1] - E[Y^0 \mid D = 0]}_{\text{Selection bias}} \\[4pt]
& - \underbrace{(ATT - ATU)}_{\text{Heterogeneous treatment effect bias}}
\end{align*}
 To mitigate these two sources of bias, the key identifying assumption is that treated and control counties are comparable before and after new casinos opened, and that treated counties are properly matched with appropriate control counties to obtain a sound estimate of the Average Treatment Effect of the Untreated. Note that while ATU is on both sides of this relationship, the source of the Heterogeneous treatment effect bias is from the difference between the ATT and the ATU; therefore, our research design entails creating an ATT most similar to the expected ATU. Second, investigate whether parallel trends hold in our analysis: absent casino openings, would treated and control counties have experienced similar population and fertility trajectories? We assess this assumption through event-study analyses and we will examine its robustness to alternative control-group definitions and trend specifications.
 
 
 
 \subsection{Addressing SUTVA concerns}
 In this section, we address SUTVA, Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption. First, treatment intensity may differ given the type of casino that opened in a given casino county. While this may be a legitimate concern, given that casino types and scales vary, the impact on family and community structures may be less significant than the casino's actual scale. We do not find prior published research documenting cases in which casino size influences social effects. Second, given our research design of using only new casinos in a treated county and excluding counties with multiple casino openings, we mitigate potential marginal changes from additional casino openings within the casino county. Third, we account for the effect of spillovers using our donut difference-in-differences design and by aggregating individual ACS data to the county level. We find it plausible that spillover effects of the casino presence become close to negligible for residents in counties twice removed from the new casino county.

\subsection{Callaway and Sant'Anna Method}

Given concerns about staggered treatment timing, we will implement the \parencite{callaway2021difference} approach. This method addresses potential bias in TWFE estimators when treatment effects vary across units or over time.

The CS estimator constructs group-time specific treatment effects $ATT(g,t)$ for cohort $g$ (year of first treatment) in period $t$:

\begin{equation}
ATT(g,t) = E[Y_t(g) - Y_t(0) | G = g]
\end{equation}

\noindent where $Y_t(g)$ is the potential outcome under treatment and $Y_t(0)$ is the potential outcome under control. The method aggregates these group-time effects into an overall average treatment effect on the treated (ATT):

\begin{equation}
ATT = \sum_{g} \sum_{t} w(g,t) \cdot ATT(g,t)
\end{equation}

\noindent where $w(g,t)$ are weights based on group sizes.

We will implement the doubly robust (DRIPW) estimator, which combines inverse probability weighting (kass, note that inverse probability weighting is just... see your theeffect book)   with outcome regression to improve efficiency and robustness. Counties that opened casinos before 2005 are excluded from the CS analysis as ``always treated'' units. However, we will not use this method in our main analysis as we believe the TWFE method is sufficient for our purposes.

\subsection{Robustness Checks}

To address potential confounding from county-specific trends, we will estimate specifications with county-specific linear time trends:

\begin{equation}
Y_{ct} = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{Post}_{ct} + \mathbf{X}_{ct}'\gamma + \delta_c \cdot t + \mu_c + \lambda_t + \varepsilon_{ct}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\delta_c \cdot t$ captures county-specific linear trends.

For the CS approach, we include county-specific trends as additional covariates in the outcome regression, allowing the method to condition on these trends when constructing treatment effect estimates.


\section{Conclusion}

This paper provides the first comprehensive evidence that casinos systematically disrupt family formation and weaken family-supporting institutions. The effects operate through both behavioral change among long-term residents and selective out-migration of family-oriented individuals. Our findings document previously unquantified social costs of casino development that should be considered in policy discussions about gambling expansion. These changes represent a fundamental shift in community character that persists and intensifies over our 8+ year observation window.
